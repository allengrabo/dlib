The Comb and the Flower

Otis Brandon’s Quiet Acts of Humanity in the Pacific War

Logline: A WWII veteran remembers that even in the aftermath of violence, a person still has choices. Through two small acts of compassion—giving food to hungry girls in Yokohama and placing a hand-drawn flower with the dead—Otis reveals a lesson larger than war: do not let brutality decide who you become.

"War is geography and guns, but humanity is a choice. True victory is refusing to lose your soul."

The Narrative Arc

The emotional and structural spine of the 8-12 minute short. The pacing deliberately contrasts the high-tension machinery of war with quiet, intimate moments of humanity.

Urban Texture: Occupied Yokohama (1945–1946)

The port city of Yokohama provides a specific visual geography for the film. In 1945, the city was a hub of logistical activity, where coalier barges unloaded fuel in sling-carried baskets and large cranes dominated the harbor basin. Amidst this industrial backdrop, civilians lived in makeshift shelters and traveled on wood-powered gas generator buses—the "wood-burners"—a critical visual detail representing ingenuity born of desperation.

Demographic Shifts: Footage should include demobilized IJA soldiers in tattered uniforms alongside "slick salarymen" and families, capturing the bitterness and suspicion that Otis chose to counter with compassion.

Materiality of Gratitude: The Kushi and Kogai

The gift of a hair comb from a young Japanese girl to Otis Brandon is the emotional center of the film. This object, which Otis has kept for eighty years, represents a "transfer of dignity" and a commitment to memory: "I'll remember you, and you remember me."

Technical Analysis of Showa-Era Ornaments

Component Visual Detail Sourcing Recommendation
Celluloid (Faux Shell) Translucent, amber plastic; fine surface scratches from 80 years of use. Macro shots with backlighting to show the material’s glow.
Makie (Gold Lacquer) Decorative gold powder depicting cranes, chrysanthemums, or blossoms. "vintage Japanese hair comb gold lacquer"
Aogai (Abalone) Iridescent shell inlays enhancing floral designs on the kushi. Macro photography to capture iridescent color shifts.
Artist Signature Often found in gold lacquer on the side or reverse. Close-up reveal to add a sense of the object’s personal history.

Symbolic Filming: The "Eternal Salute" style requires slow, tactile shots. Hands turning the comb in natural window light allows the gold lacquer to catch the eye. This creates a powerful contrast with the "machinery of war."

Infrastructure & The Sanctity of the Dead

The second act involves the death of a young girl at a railroad crossing—a fumikiri. Otis's decision to place a hand-drawn rose in her body bag perfectly encapsulates "moral survival."

Fumikiri: Railroad Infrastructure Motifs

Element Historical Detail Visual Motif for B-Roll
Manual Barrier Arm Wooden or metal arms operated by a weighted converter. Slow lowering of the gate as a train approaches.
Fumikiri-ban (Guard) Tattered uniforms; white flags signaling the tracks were "passable." Distant shot of a guard by a small railway booth.

Alabama Resilience: The Texture of Peace

Visualizing Otis's post-war life involves the specific domestic textures of the American South in the late 1940s and 1950s. This creates a contrast between ruins and renewal.

Deep Narrative Analysis: Third-Order Insights

Otis's decision represents a refusal to succumb to "moral injury"—the psychological distress from violating held moral beliefs. The visual contrast between the "body bag" and the "Alabama garden" illustrates the lifelong commitment required to maintain one's soul in the aftermath of violence.

Educational Curriculum Suite

Four distinct, trauma-informed lesson modules designed to pair with the documentary.

Social Studies Grades 8-12 | 45-60 min

Dignity in Wartime

Essential Question: Can compassion survive total war?

Module Overview

This foundational lesson shifts the focus from battlefield strategy to human choices. Students analyze oral history to distinguish between the external pressures of the military system and Otis's personal moral agency.

Key Activity

The T-Chart Analysis: While viewing, students track "External Norms" vs. "Otis's Choices".

Assessment Target

"Identify one single moment in Otis’s story that best proves that war does not erase moral choice, and explain why."

Advanced ELA / Ethics Grades 9-12 | 50-60 min

What Choices Remain?

Essential Question: How do we maintain individual values within a collective system of violence?

Module Overview

Built for discussion-heavy classrooms. This variant examines the concept of the "Enemy" and how Otis's refusal to take trophies dismantles dehumanization.

Key Activity

Four Moments Analysis: Small groups analyze the Food, Comb, Flower, and Philosophy to determine resistance to systemic pressure.

Inquiry Flow
System → Pressure → Choice → Meaning
Health / SEL Grades 8-12 | 40-50 min

How Not to Let Pain Choose

Essential Question: How can someone experience violence without turning that violence outward?

Module Overview

A trauma-informed approach focused on resilience. Frames Otis’s story as emotional discipline and kindness as a proactive survival tool.

Key Activity

The Value Map: Connect actions from the film to core values (restraint, compassion), mapping long-term impact.

"What does ‘doing the right thing’ mean when life is harsh, unfair, or painful?"

Art / English Grades 8-12 | 45-60 min

Memory Objects

Essential Question: How can ordinary objects carry moral meaning?

Module Overview

Focuses on symbolism. Students explore how simple objects hold memory and dignity more powerfully than abstract speeches.

Key Activity

Creative Response: Essay, poem, or mixed-media piece titled "An Object That Carries a Person," with an artist's statement.
